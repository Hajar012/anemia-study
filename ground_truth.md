# PART 1: OVERVIEW AND CLASSIFICATION FRAMEWORK

## 1.1 Definition of Anemia

Anemia is a condition in which the number of red blood cells (RBCs) or the level of hemoglobin (Hb) is low. Hemoglobin enables RBCs to carry oxygen from the lungs to tissues. When RBC number or Hb level is reduced, blood cannot carry adequate oxygen, causing symptoms.

## 1.2 Major Mechanisms of Anemia

Merck Manual groups anemia causes into three major mechanisms:

1. **Blood loss** (excessive bleeding)
2. **Inadequate production of red blood cells** (deficient erythropoiesis)
3. **Excessive destruction of red blood cells** (hemolysis)

## 1.3 Classification by MCV (Red Blood Cell Indices)

Merck Manual states that RBC indices—mainly mean corpuscular volume (MCV)—can narrow the differential diagnosis of deficient erythropoiesis and guide further testing.

| Category | MCV | Mechanisms/Causes |
|----------|-----|-------------------|
| **Microcytic** | Low | Deficient or defective heme or globin synthesis: iron deficiency, thalassemia, defects in hemoglobin synthesis, some anemia of chronic disease |
| **Normocytic** | 80–100 fL | Hypoproliferation due to EPO deficiency or inadequate response (inflammation, renal disease); acute blood loss; anemia of chronic disease; bone marrow disorders (aplastic anemia, pure RBC aplasia, MDS) |
| **Macrocytic** | >100 fL | Impaired DNA synthesis (vitamin B12 or folate deficiency, antineoplastic drugs, hydroxyurea, antifolate agents); alcoholism; acute bleeding (transiently due to release of large young reticulocytes); liver disease; myelodysplasia; hemolysis with reticulocytosis |

**Important:** The anemia of chronic disease may be microcytic or normocytic. Anemias due to MDS may be normocytic, macrocytic, or even microcytic.

## 1.4 Classification by Mechanism (Merck Manual Table)

Based on Merck Manual's Classification of Anemia by Cause:

### Blood Loss
- **Acute:** Childbirth, injuries, surgery
- **Chronic:** Cancer or polyps in GI tract, kidney tumors, ulcers in stomach or small intestine

### Deficient or Ineffective Erythropoiesis
- **Microcytic:** Iron-transport deficiency (IRIDA), iron utilization defect (inherited sideroblastic anemia)
- **Normochromic-Normocytic:** Kidney disease, endocrine failure (thyroid, pituitary), parvovirus B19 infection, undernutrition
- **Macrocytic:** Liver disease, malabsorption (e.g., tropical sprue), myelodysplasia

### Excessive Hemolysis Due to Extrinsically Induced RBC Abnormalities
- Reticuloendothelial hyperactivity with splenomegaly
- Immunologic abnormalities
- Drug-induced
- Infection (e.g., EBV)
- Mechanical injury (foot strike hemolysis)
- Thrombotic microangiopathies (HUS, TTP)
- Drugs/toxins (phenazopyridine, ribavirin, spider bites)

### Excessive Hemolysis Due to Intrinsic RBC Defects
- **Membrane alterations, acquired**
- **Membrane alterations, congenital:** Hereditary stomatocytosis, hereditary xerocytosis, neuroacanthocytosis
- **Metabolic disorders (inherited enzyme deficiencies):** Glycolytic pathway defects (Embden-Meyerhof pathway defects)
- **Hemoglobinopathies:** Sickle cell disease, thalassemias (beta, beta-delta, alpha)

## IRON DEFICIENCY ANEMIA

### Identity
- **Official name:** Iron Deficiency Anemia
- **Alternative names:** None specified by Merck
- **Short definition:** Results from low or depleted stores of iron, which is needed to produce red blood cells
- **Main classification(s):** Microcytic; Decreased/inadequate production; Acquired (primarily)
- **Inherited or acquired:** Acquired (excessive bleeding is most common cause)

### Cause
Excessive bleeding is the most common cause. In males and females who have stopped menstruating, iron deficiency usually indicates bleeding in the digestive tract. In females who have menstrual periods, menstrual bleeding is the most common cause. May also result from too little iron in the diet during times of increased requirements (infants, young children, adolescent females, pregnant people). Decreased iron absorption (malabsorption) can result from disorders including celiac disease.

### Pathophysiology
Iron deficiency anemia usually develops slowly because it may take several months for the body's iron reserves to be used up. As iron reserves decrease, the bone marrow gradually produces fewer red blood cells. When reserves are depleted, the red blood cells are not only fewer in number but also abnormally small and pale.

### RBC/CBC Characteristics
- **MCV:** Low (microcytic)
- **MCH/MCHC:** Low (hypochromic)
- **RBC count:** Decreased
- **Hemoglobin/Hematocrit:** Decreased
- **Reticulocyte count:** Inappropriately low (hypoproliferative)

### Peripheral Smear/Morphology
Red blood cells tend to be small and pale.

### Laboratory Findings
- **Ferritin:** ↓ Decreased (most accurate test for iron deficiency)
- **Serum iron:** ↓ Decreased (measured and compared with transferrin)
- **Transferrin/TIBC:** ↑ Increased (transferrin is the protein that carries iron when not inside RBCs)
- **Important caveat:** Ferritin levels can be falsely elevated (appear normal) due to liver damage, inflammation, infection, or cancer

### Diagnosis/Recognition
Once blood tests show anemia, tests for iron deficiency are often done. The most accurate test is measurement of blood ferritin level. A low ferritin indicates iron deficiency.

### Clinical Features
- **Common symptoms:** Fatigue, weakness, paleness (develop gradually)
- **Distinctive finding:** Many people with severe iron deficiency anemia have pica (craving to ingest something, most commonly ice, sometimes dirt, clay, or chalk)

### Treatment
- **Stopping bleeding:** First step is to locate source and stop bleeding
- **Iron supplements:** Lost iron must be replaced by taking iron supplements; correcting anemia usually takes 3 to 6 weeks
- **Administration:** Usually oral; absorbed best with least side effects when taken every other day; should not be taken with food; vitamin C may improve absorption; dairy/calcium block absorption
- **Duration:** Typically continued for 6 months after blood counts return to normal to fully replenish reserves
- **IV iron:** Sometimes given when large amounts needed or oral not tolerated

### Key Clues
- Low ferritin (most accurate test)
- Low serum iron + low transferrin saturation
- High TIBC/transferrin
- Microcytic, hypochromic RBCs
- Pica in severe cases

**Source:** Merck Manual — Iron Deficiency Anemia (Consumer Version, Apr 2026)

## ANEMIA OF CHRONIC DISEASE / INFLAMMATION

### Identity
- **Official name:** Anemia of Chronic Disease
- **Alternative names:** Anemia of Chronic Inflammation
- **Short definition:** Multifactorial anemia characterized by microcytic or normocytic anemia and low reticulocyte count, requiring an underlying chronic inflammatory disorder
- **Main classification(s):** Normocytic (early) or Microcytic (later); Decreased/inadequate production; Acquired
- **Inherited or acquired:** Acquired

### Cause
Occurs in the setting of a chronic inflammatory disorder: chronic infection, autoimmune disease (especially rheumatoid arthritis), kidney disease, heart failure, or cancer. The same process appears to begin acutely during virtually any infection or inflammation, including trauma, serious illness, or after surgery.

### Pathophysiology
Three mechanisms identified:
1. **Slightly shortened RBC survival** (probably due to increased hemophagocytosis by macrophages)
2. **Impaired erythropoiesis** due to decreased EPO production and reduced marrow responsiveness to EPO; inflammatory cytokines can impair erythroid proliferation and differentiation via free radical formation and/or apoptosis induction
3. **Altered iron metabolism** due to increased hepcidin, which inhibits iron absorption and recycling, leading to iron sequestration

Reticuloendothelial cells retain iron from senescent RBCs, making iron unavailable for hemoglobin synthesis. Thus, there is failure to compensate for anemia by increased RBC production.

### RBC/CBC Characteristics
- **MCV:** Normocytic initially; may become microcytic over time
- **Reticulocyte count:** Low

### Peripheral Smear/Morphology
Not specifically described by Merck for this entity.

### Laboratory Findings
- **Serum iron:** ↓ Decreased
- **Transferrin:** ↓ Decreased
- **Ferritin:** Normal or elevated
- **Reticulocytes:** Low

### Diagnosis/Recognition
Diagnosis requires the presence of an inflammatory chronic disease (infection, autoimmune disease, kidney disease, cancer). Should be suspected in patients with microcytic or normocytic anemia who also have a chronic disease, infection, inflammation, or cancer. Diagnosis is typically made by excluding other causes of anemia.

### Clinical Features
- Signs are generally those of the underlying disease (infection, inflammation, cancer)
- Anemia is generally mild to moderate; symptoms (fatigue, weakness, paleness) may result from the underlying disease rather than the anemia itself

### Treatment
- **Primary:** Treatment of the underlying disorder
- **Erythropoietin:** In certain cases (e.g., chronic kidney disease), EPO or darbepoetin may be given
- **Iron:** Usually given when using EPO to ensure appropriate response; iron deficiency can coexist with anemia of chronic disease

### Key Clues
- Low serum iron + low transferrin + normal/elevated ferritin
- Microcytic or normocytic anemia with low reticulocytes
- Presence of chronic inflammatory condition

**Source:** Merck Manual — Anemia of Chronic Disease (Professional, Mar 2025; Consumer, Apr 2026)

## ANEMIA OF RENAL DISEASE

### Identity
- **Official name:** Anemia of Renal Disease
- **Alternative names:** Anemia of Chronic Kidney Disease
- **Short definition:** Hypoproliferative anemia resulting primarily from EPO deficiency or decreased response to EPO; tends to be normocytic and normochromic
- **Main classification(s):** Normocytic; Decreased/inadequate production; Acquired
- **Inherited or acquired:** Acquired

### Cause
Most common mechanism is hypoproliferation due to decreased EPO production. Absence of EPO leads to loss of erythroferrone production, resulting in loss of hepcidin suppression and increased iron sequestration. Other factors include blood loss (dysfunctional platelets, dialysis, angiodysplasia), bone marrow resistance to EPO, secondary hyperparathyroidism, and uremia (shortened RBC survival).

### Pathophysiology
EPO deficiency causes loss of erythroferrone, leading to loss of hepcidin suppression and increased iron sequestration (similar to anemia of chronic disease). Anemia appears when creatinine clearance is <45 mL/min. Glomerular lesions typically cause the most severe anemia for their degree of renal failure.

### RBC/CBC Characteristics
- **MCV:** Normocytic
- **MCHC:** Normochromic
- **Reticulocyte count:** Low (reticulocytopenia)

### Peripheral Smear/Morphology
RBC fragmentation on peripheral smear (particularly with thrombocytopenia) suggests microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, in which case reticulocytes are typically high.

### Laboratory Findings
- **EPO:** ↓ Decreased (renal production)
- **Reticulocytes:** ↓ Decreased
- **Creatinine clearance:** <45 mL/min (when anemia appears)

### Diagnosis/Recognition
Diagnosis rests on demonstration of renal insufficiency, normocytic anemia, and peripheral reticulocytopenia. Bone marrow may show erythroid hypoplasia.

### Clinical Features
Those of the underlying renal disease.

### Treatment
- **Correction of underlying renal disorder:** If renal function returns to normal, anemia slowly corrects
- **Recombinant EPO:** Improves anemia and reduces transfusion needs in chronic kidney disease; generally started when Hb <9–10 g/dL in dialysis patients
- **Iron supplementation:** Given with EPO
- **In long-term dialysis:** Recombinant EPO (epoetin alfa or darbepoetin alfa) with iron supplements is treatment of choice

### Key Clues
- Normocytic anemia + low reticulocytes
- Renal insufficiency (creatinine clearance <45 mL/min)
- Low EPO

**Source:** Merck Manual — Anemia of Renal Disease (Professional, Mar 2025)

## SIDEROBLASTIC ANEMIAS

### Identity
- **Official name:** Sideroblastic Anemias
- **Alternative names:** None specified by Merck
- **Short definition:** Group of anemias characterized by increased serum iron saturation, ferritin, and transferrin, plus ring sideroblasts (erythroblasts with perinuclear mitochondria loaded with iron)
- **Main classification(s):** Microcytic (congenital); Normocytic or Macrocytic (acquired); Decreased/inadequate production; Inherited (congenital) or Acquired
- **Inherited or acquired:** Both

### Cause
**Acquired:** Frequently associated with myelodysplastic syndrome (somatic mutations in RNA splicing genes, most commonly SF3B1, occurring in adulthood); less common causes include vitamin B6 (pyridoxine) or copper deficiency (possibly caused by zinc ingestion), medications (chloramphenicol, cycloserine, isoniazid, linezolid, pyrazinamide), toxins (ethanol, zinc, lead)

**Congenital:** Caused by X-linked or autosomal mutations; most common is X-linked form caused by heterozygous mutation in ALAS2 gene

### Pathophysiology
Sideroblastic anemias are due to impaired iron utilization, characterized by inadequate mitochondrial iron use due to impaired heme synthesis, despite adequate or increased iron availability. In both acquired and congenital sideroblastic anemia, heme synthesis is impaired by inability to incorporate iron into protoporphyrin IX, inducing ring sideroblast formation.

### RBC/CBC Characteristics
- **MCV:** Microcytic and hypochromic in congenital form; normocytic or macrocytic in acquired form
- **RDW:** Typically widened due to dimorphism (variation in RBC size)
- **Reticulocyte count:** Deficient production

### Peripheral Smear/Morphology
- **Ring sideroblasts** (erythroblasts with perinuclear mitochondria loaded with iron) — diagnostic finding
- **Basophilic stippling** (siderocytes containing iron-loaded granules, Pappenheimer bodies)
- Hypochromic RBCs

### Laboratory Findings
- **Serum iron:** ↑ Increased (increased saturation)
- **Ferritin:** ↑ Increased
- **Transferrin:** ↑ Increased
- **Bone marrow:** Ring sideroblasts present

### Diagnosis/Recognition
Diagnosis requires CBC, reticulocyte count, peripheral smear, iron studies, and bone marrow examination.

### Clinical Features
- Symptoms are those of anemia: fatigue and lethargy

### Treatment
- **Stop causative substances:** zinc, certain medications, ethanol
- **Vitamin supplements**
- **Erythropoietin** or other treatments for myelodysplastic syndrome, as appropriate

### Key Clues
- Ring sideroblasts on bone marrow
- Increased serum iron + increased ferritin + increased transferrin saturation
- Basophilic stippling (Pappenheimer bodies)
- Dimorphic RBC population with widened RDW

**Source:** Merck Manual — Sideroblastic Anemias (Professional, Mar 2025)

## APLASTIC ANEMIA

### Identity
- **Official name:** Aplastic Anemia
- **Alternative names:** Hypoplastic Anemia
- **Short definition:** Disorder of the hematopoietic stem cell resulting in loss of blood cell precursors, hypoplasia or aplasia of bone marrow, and cytopenias in two or more cell lines
- **Main classification(s):** Normocytic; Decreased/inadequate production; Bone marrow/marrow disorder; Acquired or Inherited
- **Inherited or acquired:** Both (acquired most common; inherited forms exist)

### Cause
Idiopathic in approximately half of cases. Recognized causes include:
- Chemicals (benzene, inorganic arsenic)
- Medications (antineoplastic drugs, antibiotics, NSAIDs, antiseizure medications, acetazolamide, gold salts, penicillamine, quinacrine)
- Hepatitis (seronegative)
- Pregnancy
- Radiation
- Viruses (Epstein-Barr virus, cytomegalovirus)
- Inherited disorders of bone marrow failure (Fanconi anemia, Shwachman-Diamond syndrome, dyskeratosis congenita)

### Pathophysiology
Precise mechanism remains unclear, but in the majority of acquired cases, the mechanism involves an immune attack on the hematopoietic stem cell. Clonal hematopoiesis is frequently present, with risk of progression to myeloid malignancy.

### RBC/CBC Characteristics
- **MCV:** Normocytic
- **Pancytopenia:** Decreased RBCs, WBCs, and/or platelets
- **Reticulocyte count:** Decreased

### Peripheral Smear/Morphology
Pancytopenia; no specific morphological findings described by Merck.

### Laboratory Findings
- **CBC:** Pancytopenia
- **Reticulocyte count:** Decreased
- **Bone marrow biopsy:** Hypocellular marrow
- **Severe aplastic anemia:** Bone marrow <25% cellularity AND ≥2 of: ANC <500/microL, absolute reticulocyte count <60,000/microL, platelet count <20,000/microL
- **Very severe aplastic anemia:** ANC <200/microL
- **Flow cytometry:** PNH clone detection can exclude inherited syndrome
- **Genetic testing:** Consider chromosome breakage analysis (Fanconi anemia), telomere length testing

### Diagnosis/Recognition
Suspected in patients (particularly young) with pancytopenia. Diagnosis requires demonstration of peripheral pancytopenia and bone marrow biopsy revealing hypocellular marrow.

### Clinical Features
- Onset usually insidious (weeks to months after exposure)
- Anemia causes weakness and easy fatigability
- Severe thrombocytopenia: petechiae, ecchymosis, bleeding from gums, into conjunctivae
- Agranulocytosis: life-threatening infections
- Splenomegaly is absent unless induced by transfusion hemosiderosis

### Treatment
- **Immunosuppression:** Equine antithymocyte globulin and cyclosporine
- **Bone marrow transplantation**

### Key Clues
- Pancytopenia + hypocellular bone marrow
- No splenomegaly
- Reticulocytopenia

**Source:** Merck Manual — Aplastic Anemia (Professional, Mar 2025)

## MYELODYSPLASTIC SYNDROMES (MDS)

### Identity
- **Official name:** Myelodysplastic Syndromes (MDS)
- **Alternative names:** Myelodysplasia
- **Short definition:** Group of disorders characterized by peripheral blood and bone marrow morphologic abnormalities in 10–20% of cells of a particular lineage, with specific cytogenetic abnormalities and somatic mutations
- **Main classification(s):** Macrocytic (most common), but can be normocytic or microcytic; Decreased/inadequate production; Bone marrow/marrow disorder; Acquired (typically)
- **Inherited or acquired:** Acquired (typically in older adults)

### Cause
Somatic mutations and cytogenetic abnormalities; one or more clonal cytogenetic abnormalities often involving chromosomes 5 or 7.

### Pathophysiology
Ineffective hematopoiesis with morphologic abnormalities in blood and bone marrow.

### RBC/CBC Characteristics
- **MCV:** Usually macrocytic with increased RDW
- **Anemia:** Most common feature, usually associated with macrocytosis and anisocytosis
- **WBC:** May be normal, increased, or decreased
- **Platelets:** Some degree of thrombocytopenia usual; in deletion 5q syndrome, thrombocytosis is typical

### Peripheral Smear/Morphology
- Macrocytosis and anisocytosis
- Neutrophil cytoplasmic granularity decreased, with anisocytosis and variable or absent granules
- Pseudo Pelger-Huët cells (hyposegmented neutrophils) may be seen
- Platelets vary in size, some hypogranular
- Monocytosis characteristic of chronic and juvenile myelomonocytic leukemia subgroups

### Laboratory Findings
- **CBC:** Anemia, often macrocytic
- **RDW:** Increased
- **Bone marrow:** Morphologic abnormalities in 10–20% of cells of a particular lineage; cytogenetic abnormalities often involving chromosomes 5 or 7
- **Deletion 5q syndrome:** Unique form with macrocytic anemia and thrombocytosis, responsive to lenalidomide

### Diagnosis/Recognition
Suspected in patients (especially older) with refractory anemia, neutropenia, or thrombocytopenia. Diagnosis suggested by peripheral blood and bone marrow morphologic abnormalities; established by demonstrating specific cytogenetic abnormalities and somatic mutations. Must exclude cytopenias secondary to autoimmune disorders, vitamin B12/folate deficiencies, aplastic anemia, PNH, copper deficiency, zinc toxicity, or medication effects.

### Clinical Features
Symptoms reflect most affected cell line: pallor, weakness, fatigue (anemia); fever and infections (neutropenia); bruising, petechiae, epistaxis, mucosal bleeding (thrombocytopenia). Splenomegaly and hepatomegaly are not uncommon.

### Treatment
- **Supportive care:** Chronic blood and platelet transfusions for symptomatic patients
- **Iron chelation:** May benefit patients with lower-risk MDS and serum ferritin >1,000 ng/mL
- **Erythrocyte-stimulating agents (ESA):** Decrease anemia severity in 15–20% of patients (especially non-transfusion-dependent with serum EPO <500 mIU/mL)
- **ESA + G-CSF:** May increase erythroid response to nearly 40% in refractory anemia with ringed sideroblasts
- **Luspatercept:** Successful in increasing hematocrit in very low- to intermediate-risk MDS with ringed sideroblasts after ESA failure
- **Medications:** Azacitidine, decitabine, decitabine/cedazuridine
- **Allogeneic stem cell transplantation**

### Key Clues
- Macrocytic anemia + anisocytosis + increased RDW
- Pseudo Pelger-Huët cells
- Cytogenetic abnormalities (especially chromosomes 5 or 7)
- Older adult with refractory cytopenias
- Deletion 5q syndrome: macrocytic anemia + thrombocytosis, lenalidomide-responsive

**Source:** Merck Manual — Myelodysplastic Syndromes (MDS) (Professional, Feb 2026)

## THALASSEMIAS

### Identity
- **Official name:** Thalassemias
- **Alternative names:** Mediterranean anemia; thalassemia major and minor
- **Short definition:** Group of inherited diseases due to disruption in manufacturing one of the four amino acid chains that compose hemoglobin
- **Main classification(s):** Microcytic; Genetic/inherited; Hemolytic; Decreased effective production
- **Inherited or acquired:** Inherited

### Cause
Genetic mutations affecting alpha or beta globin chain synthesis. Alpha-thalassemia more common in people of African, Mediterranean, or Southeast Asian origin. Beta-thalassemia more common in people of Mediterranean, Indian, Middle Eastern, or Southeast Asian origin.

### Pathophysiology
Hemoglobin is composed of two pairs of globin chains. Normally, adults have one pair of alpha chains and one pair of beta chains. In thalassemia, one or more of these chains are reduced or absent. Classification is based on the affected chain: alpha-thalassemia (alpha chain abnormality) or beta-thalassemia (beta chain abnormality).

### RBC/CBC Characteristics
- **MCV:** Microcytic (low)
- **MCH:** Hypochromic
- **Reticulocyte count:** Variable depending on severity

### Peripheral Smear/Morphology
- **Target cells (dianocytes):** Thin RBCs with a central dot of hemoglobin, due to imbalance between cell volume and hemoglobin content; characteristic of thalassemia but also occur in other hemoglobinopathies (Hb C, Hb S-C), after splenectomy, and in liver disease
- Microcytosis, hypochromia

### Laboratory Findings
- **Hemoglobin electrophoresis:** Diagnostic; shows specific globin chain abnormalities

### Diagnosis/Recognition
Diagnosis generally requires specific hemoglobin testing. Classification by severity: thalassemia minor/trait (asymptomatic or mild anemia, no transfusion needed); thalassemia major (severe forms with markedly decreased globin production and severe anemia requiring transfusions).

### Clinical Features
**Alpha-thalassemia minor and beta-thalassemia minor:** Discrete asymptomatic anemia

**Alpha-thalassemia major:** Moderate to severe anemia, fatigue, shortness of breath, pallor, splenomegaly with abdominal bloating and discomfort. If alpha chains absent, fetus develops severe anemia, heart failure, and anasarca (hydrops fetalis). Without intrauterine transfusions, many fetuses die before birth or are stillborn.

**Beta-thalassemia major (Cooley anemia):** Severe anemia symptoms (fatigue, weakness, shortness of breath), jaundice, skin ulcers, gallstones. Splenomegaly. Bone marrow hyperactivity causes bone thickening and enlargement (especially head and face). Long bones weakened and fracture easily. Growth and puberty delays in children. Increased iron absorption and frequent transfusions lead to iron overload, with iron deposition in heart muscle causing iron overload syndrome.

### Treatment
- **Mild thalassemia:** May not require treatment
- **Severe thalassemia:** May require bone marrow transplantation
- **Transfusion-dependent forms:** Regular transfusions
- **Iron chelation:** For transfusional iron overload

### Key Clues
- Microcytic anemia + target cells on smear
- Elevated Hb A2 or Hb F on electrophoresis (beta-thalassemia)
- Family history / ethnic background
- Normal or increased iron stores (distinguishes from iron deficiency)

**Source:** Merck Manual — Thalassemias (Consumer, Apr 2026)

## SICKLE CELL DISEASE

### Identity
- **Official name:** Sickle Cell Disease
- **Alternative names:** Sickle cell anemia (for Hb SS)
- **Short definition:** Lifelong inherited disorder causing some red blood cells to be sickle-shaped (crescent-shaped) instead of disk-shaped
- **Main classification(s):** Normocytic; Genetic/inherited; Hemolytic; Hemoglobinopathy
- **Inherited or acquired:** Inherited (autosomal recessive)

### Cause
Caused by genes passed from parents. Need two sickle cell genes (one from each parent) to have sickle cell disease. Sickle cell trait occurs with only one copy of the sickle cell gene and one normal copy.

### Pathophysiology
Abnormal hemoglobin (HbS) causes RBCs to become rigid and sickle-shaped, leading to:
- Chronic hemolytic anemia
- Vaso-occlusive events resulting in tissue ischemia, infarction, and inflammation

### RBC/CBC Characteristics
- **MCV:** Normocytic (microcytosis suggests concomitant alpha or beta thalassemia)
- **Reticulocyte count:** Reticulocytosis ≥10% is common
- **Nucleated RBCs:** Frequently appear in peripheral blood

### Peripheral Smear/Morphology
- **Sickle cells:** Crescent-shaped RBCs, often with elongated or pointed ends, seen on dry-stained smears
- Nucleated RBCs

### Laboratory Findings
- **Hemoglobin electrophoresis:** Homozygous state shows only Hb S with variable Hb F; heterozygous state shows more Hb A than Hb S
- **Solubility testing:** Positive
- **DNA testing:** For prenatal diagnosis or confirmation
- **Serum bilirubin:** Usually elevated (e.g., 2–4 mg/dL)
- **Reticulocyte count <1%:** Suggests aplastic crisis, particularly when Hb decreases below patient's usual level
- **Bone marrow:** Not used for diagnosis; if done, shows hyperplasia with normoblasts predominating

### Diagnosis/Recognition
Patients with symptoms or signs suggesting the disorder or its complications (poor growth, acute unexplained bone pain, aseptic necrosis of femoral head, unexplained hematuria), and patients with African ancestry and normocytic anemia (particularly with hemolysis) require laboratory tests for hemolytic anemia, hemoglobin electrophoresis, and examination of RBCs for sickling.

### Clinical Features
**Most symptoms result from:** Anemia and vaso-occlusive events
- Anemia can be severe but varies among patients and genotypes; usually compensated; mild jaundice and pallor common
- Hepatosplenomegaly common in children; spleen commonly atrophied in adults due to repeated infarctions and fibrosis (autosplenectomy)
- Cardiomegaly and systolic ejection murmurs common
- Cholelithiasis and chronic punched-out skin ulcers around ankles
- **Painful vaso-occlusive crisis:** Severe pain in long bones, hands and feet, back, and joints
- **Acute chest syndrome:** Sudden onset of fever, chest pain, pulmonary infiltrates; may follow bacterial pneumonia; hypoxemia may develop rapidly

### Treatment
- **Analgesics and IV hydration** for vaso-occlusive pain crisis
- **Transfusion:** Simple transfusion to correct anemia (e.g., during aplastic crisis); exchange transfusion during severe acute events (acute chest syndrome, stroke) to decrease Hb S percentage
- **Chronic transfusion therapy:** Indicated for prevention of recurrent cerebral thrombosis, especially in children, to maintain Hb S <30%
- **Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation:** Only curative treatment; traditionally with matched sibling donor, increasingly using alternative donors

### Key Clues
- Sickle cells on peripheral smear
- HbS on hemoglobin electrophoresis
- Reticulocytosis ≥10%
- Vaso-occlusive crises
- Normocytic anemia with hemolysis
- African ancestry

**Source:** Merck Manual — Sickle Cell Disease (Professional, Mar 2026)

## HEREDITARY SPHEROCYTOSIS AND HEREDITARY ELLIPTOCYTOSIS

### Identity
- **Official name:** Hereditary Spherocytosis and Hereditary Elliptocytosis
- **Alternative names (HS):** Chronic familial jaundice; congenital hemolytic jaundice; familial spherocytosis; spherocytic anemia
- **Short definition:** Congenital disorders of the RBC membrane causing mild hemolytic anemia
- **Main classification(s):** Normocytic; Hemolytic; Genetic/inherited; Membrane disorder
- **Inherited or acquired:** Inherited

### Cause
Hereditary spherocytosis is an autosomal dominant disease with variable gene penetrance due to mutations in spectrin, ankyrin, band 3, or protein 4.2. Approximately 25% of cases are sporadic (de novo dominant mutation) or autosomal recessive (rare).

### Pathophysiology
In both disorders, RBC abnormalities are due to alterations in membrane proteins. In hereditary spherocytosis, there is disproportionate decrease in cell membrane surface area relative to intracellular content due to loss of membrane-associated proteins. The smaller cell surface alters the flexibility needed for the cell to pass through splenic microcirculation, causing intrasplenic hemolysis.

### RBC/CBC Characteristics
- **MCV:** Normal (spherocytes are spheroids with normal MCV, but mean corpuscular diameter is less than normal)
- **Reticulocyte count:** Increased (reticulocytosis)

### Peripheral Smear/Morphology
- **Spherocytes** (for hereditary spherocytosis)
- Elliptocytes (for hereditary elliptocytosis)

### Laboratory Findings
- **Osmotic fragility:** Increased (diagnostic)
- **Direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test:** Negative
- **Genetic testing:** Often performed

### Diagnosis/Recognition
Suspected in patients with hemolysis of unknown cause (suggested by anemia and reticulocytosis), particularly if splenomegaly, family history of similar manifestations, or suggestive RBC indices are present. Diagnosis requires demonstration of increased osmotic fragility and a negative direct antiglobulin test.

### Clinical Features
- Symptoms and signs usually mild
- Anemia may be so well compensated that it is unrecognized until an intercurrent viral illness (e.g., parvovirus infection) transiently decreases RBC production and causes aplastic crisis
- Jaundice and moderate symptoms in severe cases
- **Splenomegaly:** Almost invariable; rarely causes abdominal discomfort
- Hepatomegaly may be present
- **Cholelithiasis (pigment stones):** Frequent and may be the presenting sign

### Treatment
- **Splenectomy:** Rarely, patients <45 years with symptomatic disease require splenectomy

### Key Clues
- Spherocytes on peripheral smear
- Increased osmotic fragility
- Negative direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test
- Splenomegaly
- Family history
- Reticulocytosis

**Source:** Merck Manual — Hereditary Spherocytosis and Hereditary Elliptocytosis (Professional, Apr 2026)

## GLUCOSE-6-PHOSPHATE DEHYDROGENASE (G6PD) DEFICIENCY

### Identity
- **Official name:** Glucose-6-Phosphate Dehydrogenase (G6PD) Deficiency
- **Alternative names:** Favism (when triggered by fava beans)
- **Short definition:** Inherited enzyme deficiency causing episodic hemolytic anemia triggered by oxidative stress
- **Main classification(s):** Normocytic (during hemolysis); Hemolytic; Genetic/inherited; Enzyme disorder
- **Inherited or acquired:** Inherited (X-linked)

### Cause
X-linked genetic deficiency of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase, an enzyme in the hexose monophosphate shunt pathway that protects RBCs from oxidative damage.

### Pathophysiology
G6PD deficiency renders RBCs vulnerable to oxidative stress. When exposed to oxidant drugs, infections, or fava beans, hemoglobin oxidizes and precipitates as Heinz bodies. RBCs with Heinz bodies are removed by the spleen, causing intravascular and extravascular hemolysis.

### RBC/CBC Characteristics
- **MCV:** Normocytic (or slightly increased due to reticulocytosis)
- **Reticulocyte count:** Increased during hemolytic episodes
- **Hemoglobin:** Falls abruptly during acute hemolysis

### Peripheral Smear/Morphology
- **Bite cells (degmacytes):** RBCs with a bite-like defect where Heinz bodies were removed by splenic macrophages
- **Heinz bodies:** Denatured hemoglobin inclusions (best seen with supravital staining)
- **Blister cells:** RBCs with vesicles

### Laboratory Findings
- **G6PD enzyme activity:** ↓ Decreased (diagnostic; must be interpreted carefully during hemolytic episodes because reticulocytes have higher G6PD activity)
- **Bilirubin:** ↑ Increased (indirect)
- **LDH:** ↑ Increased
- **Haptoglobin:** ↓ Decreased
- **Direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test:** Negative

### Diagnosis/Recognition
Suspected in patients with acute hemolysis after exposure to oxidant drugs, infection, or fava beans, particularly in individuals of African, Mediterranean, or Southeast Asian descent. Diagnosis confirmed by measuring G6PD enzyme activity; testing should be repeated after resolution of hemolysis if initially normal.

### Clinical Features
- Usually asymptomatic between episodes
- Acute hemolytic episode: jaundice, dark urine (hemoglobinuria), pallor, fatigue, back pain
- Severe cases: cardiovascular collapse, acute kidney injury

### Treatment
- **Avoidance of triggers:** Oxidant drugs (e.g., primaquine, dapsone, nitrofurantoin, sulfonamides), fava beans, naphthalene
- **Supportive care:** Hydration, transfusion for severe anemia
- **No specific therapy** to correct the enzyme deficiency

### Key Clues
- Bite cells and Heinz bodies on smear
- Acute hemolysis after oxidant exposure
- Negative Coombs test
- X-linked inheritance
- Ethnic background (African, Mediterranean, Southeast Asian)

**Source:** Merck Manual — Glucose-6-Phosphate Dehydrogenase (G6PD) Deficiency (Professional, Mar 2025)

## AUTOIMMUNE HEMOLYTIC ANEMIA

### Identity
- **Official name:** Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia (AIHA)
- **Alternative names:** Warm antibody hemolytic anemia (most common form); cold agglutinin disease (cold antibody disease); paroxysmal cold hemoglobinuria (PCH; Donath-Landsteiner syndrome) — rare subtype
- **Short definition:** Group of disorders characterized by malfunction of the immune system, resulting in production of autoantibodies that attack red blood cells as if they were substances foreign to the body
- **Main classification(s):** Normocytic; Hemolytic; Acquired; Immunologic
- **Inherited or acquired:** Acquired

### Cause
Caused by autoantibodies that react with red blood cells at temperatures ≥ 37° C (warm antibody hemolytic anemia) or < 37° C (cold agglutinin disease). Hemolysis is predominantly extravascular.

**Warm antibody hemolytic anemia (most common form of AIHA):**
- Autoantibodies react at temperatures ≥ 37° C
- Classified as Primary (idiopathic) or Secondary (occurring in association with an underlying disorder such as systemic lupus erythematosus, lymphoma, or chronic lymphocytic leukemia, or after use of certain medications)
- Some medications (eg, methyldopa, levodopa) stimulate production of autoantibodies against Rh antigens (methyldopa-type of AIHA)
- Other medications stimulate production of autoantibodies against an antibiotic–RBC-membrane complex as part of a transient hapten mechanism; the hapten may be stable (eg, high-dose penicillin, cephalosporins) or unstable (eg, quinidine, sulfonamides)

**Cold agglutinin disease:**
- Caused by autoantibodies that react at temperatures < 37° C; some autoantibodies have a higher thermal amplitude (eg, > 30° C most likely to cause clinical manifestations)
- Antibody thermal amplitude is more important than its titer
- Primary or idiopathic: usually associated with a monoclonal B-cell population in individuals considered to have a low-grade lymphoproliferative disorder; antibodies usually directed against the I antigen
- Secondary cold agglutinin syndrome: occurs in the setting of an underlying disorder such as infections (especially mycoplasmal pneumonias or infectious mononucleosis, with antibodies directed against the I [mycoplasma] or i [Epstein-Barr virus] antigens) or an overt lymphoma
- Infections tend to cause acute disease; idiopathic disease (the common form in older adults) tends to be chronic

**Paroxysmal cold hemoglobinuria (PCH):**
- Rare type of cold reactive AIHA; more common in children
- Hemolysis results from exposure to cold, which may even be localized (eg, from drinking cold water, from washing hands in cold water)
- An IgG antibody binds to the P antigen on RBCs at low temperatures and causes intravascular hemolysis and hemoglobinuria after warming
- Occurs most often after a nonspecific viral illness or in otherwise healthy patients, although it occurs in some patients with congenital or acquired syphilis

### Pathophysiology
Autoimmune hemolytic anemia is caused by abnormalities extrinsic to the red blood cell. 

**Warm antibody hemolytic anemia:** Hemolysis occurs primarily in the spleen and is not due to direct lysis of RBCs. It may be severe and can be fatal. Most of the autoantibodies are IgG. Most are polyclonal panagglutinins against common RBC antigens.

**Cold agglutinin disease:** The chronic hemolysis occurs largely in the extravascular mononuclear phagocyte system of the liver and spleen, but intravascular hemolysis can occur when a complement-amplifying condition, such as infection, is present. The anemia is usually non-severe. Autoantibodies in cold agglutinin disease are almost always clonal IgM.

**Paroxysmal cold hemoglobinuria:** IgG antibody binds to the P antigen on RBCs at low temperatures and causes intravascular hemolysis and hemoglobinuria after warming. The severity and rapidity of development of the anemia varies and may be fulminant.

### RBC/CBC Characteristics
- **MCV:** Normocytic; a high MCV may occur due to extreme reticulocytosis or agglutination in cold agglutinin disease
- **Reticulocyte count:** High (reticulocytosis) in warm AIHA and cold agglutinin disease; in cold agglutinin disease when unwarmed blood is used, automated cell counts often reveal an increased MCV and spuriously low hemoglobin due to RBC clumping (hand warming of the tube and recounting results in values significantly closer to normal)
- **Hemoglobin:** Decreased; may be spuriously low in cold agglutinin disease due to clumping

### Peripheral Smear/Morphology
- **Spherocytes (microspherocytes):** Usually seen in warm AIHA; spherical red blood cells without an area of central pallor, usually slightly smaller than the average red cell; have increased osmotic fragility
- **Few or no schistocytes:** In warm AIHA, indicating extravascular hemolysis
- **RBC clumping:** In cold agglutinin disease when unwarmed blood is used, RBCs clump on the peripheral smear

### Laboratory Findings
- **Direct antiglobulin (direct Coombs) test:** Positive; establishes the diagnosis and may suggest the cause
  - In warm antibody hemolytic anemia: IgG is nearly always present, and C3 (C3b and C3d) may be present as well
  - In cold antibody disease: C3 is present while IgG is usually absent
  - Three patterns are possible:
    1. Positive with anti-IgG and negative with anti-C3 — common in idiopathic AIHA and drug-associated/methyldopa-type AIHA (usually warm)
    2. Positive with anti-IgG and anti-C3 — common in SLE and idiopathic AIHA (usually warm); rare in drug-associated cases
    3. Positive with anti-C3 but negative with anti-IgG — occurs in cold agglutinin disease (antibody most commonly IgM); can also occur in warm AIHA when the IgG antibody is of low affinity, in some drug-associated cases, and in PCH
- **Indirect antiglobulin (indirect Coombs) test:** Complementary; a positive indirect test and negative direct test generally indicate an alloantibody caused by pregnancy, prior transfusions, or lectin cross-reactivity rather than immune hemolysis
- **LDH:** Elevated (hemolysis)
- **Indirect bilirubin:** Elevated (hemolysis)
- **Haptoglobin:** Decreased (hemolysis)
- **Cold agglutinin titer:** A titer of 1:64 is typically part of the diagnosis, though usually titers are higher and occasionally the disease is diagnosed at lower titers
- **Thermal amplitude testing (cold agglutinin disease):** Measures the temperature range in which a cold autoantibody binds to its antigen; cold antibodies that can bind to antigen above 28 to 30° C are considered potentially clinically significant
- **Donath-Landsteiner test (if PCH suspected):** Specific for PCH; patient's serum is incubated with normal RBCs at 4° C for 30 minutes to allow fixation of complement, then warmed to body temperature; hemolysis of RBCs during this test is indicative of PCH
- **Important caveats:** The direct antiglobulin test is highly sensitive for AIHA with an estimated ~5% of AIHA cases being direct antiglobulin test-negative (false-negative results can occur if antibody density is very low or, rarely, if the autoantibodies are IgA). The direct antiglobulin test may also be positive in the absence of AIHA (false-positive results may result from clinically insignificant antibodies, elevated paraproteins, IV immune globulin, RhD immunoglobulin, daratumumab therapy, or alloantibodies after recent transfusion and delayed hemolytic transfusion reaction). Approximately 1/10,000 healthy blood donors has a positive test result.

### Diagnosis/Recognition
Autoimmune hemolytic anemia should be suspected in any patient with a hemolytic anemia (as suggested by the presence of anemia and reticulocytosis).

**Diagnostic tests include:** Peripheral smear, reticulocyte count, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), haptoglobin, indirect bilirubin, direct antiglobulin test. Cold agglutinin titer and thermal amplitude testing in cold agglutinin disease.

In warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia, the peripheral smear usually shows microspherocytes and a high reticulocyte count with few or no schistocytes, indicating extravascular hemolysis. Laboratory tests typically indicate hemolysis (eg, elevated LDH and indirect bilirubin and decreased haptoglobin).

The presence of abundant spherocytes in the peripheral blood smear suggests either hereditary spherocytosis (HS) or autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA). The osmotic fragility test is positive in both HS and AIHA. The direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test distinguishes between them; the result is positive in AIHA and negative in HS.

Once AIHA has been identified by the antiglobulin test, testing should differentiate between warm antibody hemolytic anemia and cold agglutinin disease as well as the mechanism responsible for warm antibody hemolytic anemia. This determination can often be made by observing the pattern of the direct antiglobulin reaction.

Other tests can suggest the cause of AIHA but are not definitive. In cold agglutinin disease, when unwarmed blood is used, RBCs clump on the peripheral smear and automated cell counts often reveal an increased MCV and spuriously low hemoglobin.

Hemolytic anemia in the setting of a low reticulocyte count is rare but can occur due to factors such as renal insufficiency, infection, or bone marrow failure and constitutes a medical emergency requiring prompt transfusions.

### Clinical Features
**General (anemia-related):** Some people have no symptoms, especially when destruction is mild and develops gradually. Others have symptoms similar to other types of anemia: fatigue, weakness, paleness. Symptoms of severe or rapid destruction may include jaundice, fever, chest pain, fainting, symptoms of heart failure (eg, shortness of breath), and even death.

**Warm antibody hemolytic anemia:**
- Symptoms tend to be due to the anemia
- If severe: fever, chest pain, syncope, or liver or heart failure may occur
- Mild splenomegaly is typical
- Venous thromboembolic events are common

**Cold agglutinin disease:**
- Manifests as an acute or chronic hemolytic anemia
- Acrocyanosis, Raynaud phenomenon, cold-associated occlusive changes
- In the hands and feet: may appear bluish or grayish, develop ulcers, and become painful
- Associated with elevated thrombotic risk

**Paroxysmal cold hemoglobinuria:**
- Severe pain in the back and legs, headache, vomiting, diarrhea, passage of dark brown urine
- Hepatosplenomegaly may be present
- In children, this disease is often self-resolving

**When caused by another disorder:** Symptoms of the underlying disorder (eg, swollen and tender lymph nodes or fever) may dominate.

### Treatment
- **Blood transfusion:** Most important treatment for patients who have symptoms and who rapidly develop severe, life-threatening anemia; should never be withheld due to lack of "compatible" units. In general, patients who have not had a previous blood transfusion or been pregnant are at low risk for hemolysis of ABO-compatible blood. Even if transfused cells are hemolyzed, blood transfusion can be life-saving until more definitive therapy can be done.
- **For drug-induced warm antibody hemolytic anemia:** Drug withdrawal and sometimes IV immune globulin (IVIG)
- **For idiopathic warm antibody hemolytic anemia:** Glucocorticoids (eg, prednisone — high doses at first, followed by gradual reduction over many weeks or months); in refractory cases, rituximab, IVIG, immunosuppression (eg, with azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, or cyclophosphamide), or splenectomy
- **For cold agglutinin disease:** Avoidance of cold and treatment of underlying disorder; sometimes rituximab with or without chemotherapy, or complement inhibitors
- **For PCH:** Avoidance of cold, immunosuppressants, and treatment of syphilis if present. In children, this disease is often self-resolving.
- **Erythropoietin:** May be given if the reticulocyte response is inadequate
- **Splenectomy rationale:** The spleen is removed because it is the primary place where antibody-coated red blood cells are destroyed

### Key Clues
- Positive direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test
- Spherocytes on peripheral smear (warm AIHA)
- Elevated LDH + elevated indirect bilirubin + decreased haptoglobin (hemolysis)
- Reticulocytosis (unless concomitant marrow failure/renal insufficiency/infection)
- Negative direct antiglobulin test distinguishes from hereditary spherocytosis (which also has spherocytes and positive osmotic fragility)
- Cold agglutinin disease: RBC clumping on smear, acrocyanosis, Raynaud phenomenon, high MCV (spurious)
- PCH: Donath-Landsteiner test positive; dark brown urine after cold exposure; IgG against P antigen

**Source:** Merck Manual — Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia (Professional, Apr 2026; Consumer, Apr 2024)

## VITAMIN B12 DEFICIENCY

### Identity
- **Official name:** Vitamin B12 Deficiency
- **Alternative names:** Cobalamin deficiency; pernicious anemia (specifically refers to anemia resulting from vitamin B12 deficiency caused by an autoimmune metaplastic atrophic gastritis with loss of intrinsic factor)
- **Short definition:** Deficiency of vitamin B12 causing megaloblastic anemia, damage to the white matter of the spinal cord and brain, and peripheral neuropathy
- **Main classification(s):** Macrocytic (megaloblastic); Decreased/inadequate production; Acquired (most common) or Inherited (rare genetic malabsorption)
- **Inherited or acquired:** Acquired (most common). Inherited: Imerslund-Graesbeck syndrome (genetic disorder causing malabsorption in the ileum)

### Cause
Vitamin B12 deficiency can result from:
- **Inadequate intake** — possible in vegans but otherwise unlikely. Breastfed babies of vegan mothers may develop deficiency by age 4–6 months because their liver stores are limited and rapid growth increases demand.
- **Inadequate absorption** — most common cause. In older adults, most commonly results from decreased acid secretion (crystalline vitamin B12 can be absorbed, but food-bound B12 is not liberated normally). May occur in small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (blind loop syndrome), fish tapeworm infestation, inflammatory bowel disease, surgical removal of ileal absorptive sites, chronic pancreatitis, gastric or bariatric surgery, malabsorption syndromes, advanced HIV infection, use of certain medications (eg, antacids, metformin), repeated exposure to nitrous oxide, and Imerslund-Graesbeck syndrome.
- **Decreased utilization**
- **Use of certain medications**

Pernicious anemia specifically refers to anemia resulting from vitamin B12 deficiency caused by an autoimmune metaplastic atrophic gastritis with loss of intrinsic factor. Patients with classic pernicious anemia, most commonly younger adults, are at increased risk of stomach and other gastrointestinal cancers.

### Pathophysiology
Cobalamin compounds are involved in nucleic acid metabolism, methyl transfer, and myelin synthesis and repair. They are necessary for the formation of normal red blood cells and normal neural function.

Food-bound vitamin B12 is released in the stomach's acidic environment and bound to R protein (haptocorrin). Pancreatic enzymes cleave this B12 complex in the small intestine. After cleavage, intrinsic factor (secreted by parietal cells in the gastric mucosa) binds with vitamin B12. Intrinsic factor is required for absorption of vitamin B12, which takes place in the terminal ileum. Vitamin B12 in plasma is bound to transcobalamins I and II; transcobalamin II delivers vitamin B12 to tissues. The liver stores large amounts of vitamin B12. Liver stores can normally sustain physiologic needs for 3–5 years if B12 intake stops (eg, in people who become vegans) and for months to 1 year if enterohepatic reabsorption capacity is absent.

Megaloblastic states result from defective DNA synthesis. RNA synthesis continues, resulting in a large cell with a large nucleus. All cell lines have dyspoiesis (cytoplasmic maturity greater than nuclear maturity), producing megaloblasts in the marrow before they appear in the peripheral blood. Dyspoiesis results in intramedullary cell death, making erythropoiesis ineffective.

### RBC/CBC Characteristics
- **MCV:** > 100 fL (macrocytic) in the absence of iron deficiency, thalassemia trait, or renal disease
- **RDW:** High (increased RBC distribution width)
- **Reticulocyte count:** Reticulocytopenia
- **Hemoglobin/Hematocrit:** Decreased
- **WBC:** Neutropenia develops later
- **Platelets:** Thrombocytopenia is often present in severe cases; platelets may be bizarre in size and shape
- **Important caveat:** If iron deficiency or impaired iron utilization or renal disease are present simultaneously, macrocytosis may not develop. Severe neurologic disease may occur without anemia or macrocytosis.

### Peripheral Smear/Morphology
- **Macro-ovalocytes:** Large oval red blood cells
- **Anisocytosis:** Variation in RBC size
- **Poikilocytosis:** Variation in RBC shape
- **Hypersegmented neutrophils:** Hypersegmentation of polymorphonuclear neutrophils develops early
- **Howell-Jolly bodies:** Residual fragments of the nucleus, usually present
- **Important caveat:** Although iron deficiency can mask macrocytosis, Howell-Jolly bodies and granulocyte hypersegmentation should still be present

### Laboratory Findings
- **Serum vitamin B12:** ↓ Decreased. A level < 200 pg/mL (< 145 pmol/L) indicates deficiency. Levels between 200 and 350 pg/mL (145–260 pmol/L) are nondiagnostic / low-normal.
- **Serum folate:** Measured to differentiate from folate deficiency
- **Methylmalonic acid (MMA):** ↑ Elevated in vitamin B12 deficiency. May also be elevated in renal failure. Remains normal in folate deficiency. Can be used to monitor response to treatment.
- **Homocysteine:** ↑ Elevated (may be elevated with either vitamin B12 or folate deficiency)
- **Holotranscobalamin II:** ↓ When < 40 pg/mL (< 30 pmol/L), vitamin B12 is deficient
- **Schilling test:** Used to determine etiology (especially intrinsic factor deficiency / classic pernicious anemia). If absorption is normal, ≥ 9% of the radiolabeled dose appears in the urine. Reduced urinary excretion (< 5% if kidney function is normal) indicates inadequate absorption. Improved absorption with added intrinsic factor confirms pernicious anemia. Test is often difficult to do or interpret; does not measure absorption of protein-bound B12.
- **Serum gastrin / intrinsic factor autoantibodies:** May be measured; sensitivity and specificity may be poor

### Diagnosis/Recognition
Diagnosis is based on CBC and vitamin B12 and folate levels. CBC usually detects megaloblastic anemia. Tissue deficiency and macrocytic indexes may precede the development of anemia. The folate level is measured because vitamin B12 deficiency must be differentiated from folate deficiency as a cause of megaloblastic anemia. When clinical judgment suggests vitamin B12 deficiency but the B12 level is low-normal or hematologic indexes are normal, measure methylmalonic acid, homocysteine, or holotranscobalamin II. After diagnosis, additional tests (eg, Schilling test) may be indicated for young and middle-aged adults but usually not for older adults.

### Clinical Features
**General (anemia-related):** Anemia usually develops insidiously. It is often more severe than its symptoms indicate because its slow evolution allows physiologic adaptation. Occasionally, splenomegaly and hepatomegaly occur. Various gastrointestinal symptoms, including weight loss and poorly localized abdominal pain, may occur. Glossitis, usually described as burning of the tongue, is uncommon.

**Neurologic (unique to B12 deficiency):**
- Neurologic symptoms develop independently from and often without hematologic abnormalities
- Subacute combined degeneration: degenerative changes affecting mostly brain and spinal cord white matter
- Demyelinating or axonal peripheral neuropathies
- Early stages: decreased position and vibratory sensation in the extremities, mild to moderate weakness, hyporeflexia
- Later stages: spasticity, extensor plantar responses, greater loss of position and vibratory sensation in the lower extremities, ataxia
- Deficits may develop in a stocking-glove distribution
- Tactile, pain, and temperature sensations are usually spared but may be difficult to assess in older adults
- Some patients are irritable and mildly depressed
- Paranoia (megaloblastic madness), delirium, confusion, and postural hypotension may occur in advanced cases
- Paresthesias can be the presenting manifestation, even before (or in the absence of) anemia

### Treatment
- **Vitamin B12 1000–2000 mcg orally once a day:** Can be given to patients who do not have severe deficiency or neurologic symptoms or signs. Large oral doses can be absorbed by mass action, even when intrinsic factor is absent.
- **Nasal gel preparation:** Available at a higher price
- **For more severe deficiency:** Vitamin B12 1 mg IM 1–4 times a week for several weeks until hematologic abnormalities are corrected; then once a month
- **Monitoring:** If the methylmalonic acid level does not decrease, patients may not be taking vitamin B12
- **Duration:** Hematologic abnormalities usually corrected within 6 weeks (reticulocyte count should improve within 1 week). Resolution of neurologic symptoms may take much longer. Neurologic symptoms persisting for months or years become irreversible. In most older adults with B12 deficiency and dementia, cognition does not improve after treatment.
- **Lifelong treatment:** Must be continued for life unless the pathophysiologic mechanism is corrected
- **Infants of vegan mothers:** Should receive supplemental vitamin B12 from birth
- **CRITICAL CAUTION:** Folate should not be used instead of vitamin B12 because folate may alleviate the anemia but allow neurologic deficits to progress.

### Key Clues
- Macrocytosis (MCV > 100 fL) + hypersegmented neutrophils + macro-ovalocytes
- Low serum vitamin B12 (< 200 pg/mL)
- Elevated methylmalonic acid (distinguishes from folate deficiency)
- Neurologic symptoms (subacute combined degeneration, peripheral neuropathy) — unique to B12
- Paresthesias may be the presenting manifestation
- Schilling test confirms pernicious anemia
- Pancytopenia in severe cases

**Source:** Merck Manual — Vitamin B12 Deficiency (Professional, Jun 2026) and Megaloblastic Macrocytic Anemias (Professional, Mar 2025)

## FOLATE DEFICIENCY

### Identity
- **Official name:** Folate Deficiency
- **Alternative names:** Folic acid deficiency
- **Short definition:** Deficiency of folate causing megaloblastic anemia (indistinguishable from that due to vitamin B12 deficiency) and, in pregnancy, increased risk of neural tube birth defects
- **Main classification(s):** Macrocytic (megaloblastic); Decreased/inadequate production; Acquired
- **Inherited or acquired:** Acquired

### Cause
The most common causes of folate deficiency are:
- **Inadequate intake** — usually in patients with undernutrition or alcohol use disorder
- **Increased demand** — eg, due to pregnancy or lactation; also chronic hemolysis (ie, hemoglobinopathies)
- **Impaired absorption** — eg, in celiac disease, tropical sprue, short bowel syndrome, or due to certain medications
- **Inadequate bioavailability** and increased excretion

Prolonged cooking destroys folate, predisposing to inadequate intake. Intake is sometimes barely adequate (eg, in people with alcohol use disorder). Liver stores provide only a several-month supply. Alcohol interferes with folate absorption, metabolism, renal excretion, and enterohepatic reabsorption and reduces healthy food intake.

Medications that impair folate metabolism: 5-fluorouracil, metformin, methotrexate, phenobarbital, phenytoin, triamterene, trimethoprim.

Folate deficiency is exceedingly rare in countries where cereals and grains are fortified with folic acid.

### Pathophysiology
Folates are involved in red blood cell maturation and synthesis of purines and pyrimidines. They are required for development of the fetal nervous system. Absorption occurs in the duodenum and upper jejunum. Enterohepatic circulation of folate occurs.

Megaloblastic states result from defective DNA synthesis. RNA synthesis continues, resulting in a large cell with a large nucleus. All cell lines have dyspoiesis (cytoplasmic maturity greater than nuclear maturity), producing megaloblasts in the marrow before they appear in the peripheral blood. Dyspoiesis results in intramedullary cell death, making erythropoiesis ineffective. Because dyspoiesis affects all cell lines, reticulocytopenia and, during later stages, leukopenia and thrombocytopenia develop.

### RBC/CBC Characteristics
- **MCV:** > 100 fL (macrocytic) — megaloblastic anemia indistinguishable from that of vitamin B12 deficiency
- **RDW:** High (increased)
- **Reticulocyte count:** Reticulocytopenia
- **Hemoglobin/Hematocrit:** Decreased
- **WBC:** Neutropenia may develop
- **Platelets:** Thrombocytopenia may develop in severe cases

### Peripheral Smear/Morphology
- **Macro-ovalocytes:** Large oval red blood cells
- **Anisocytosis:** Variation in RBC size
- **Poikilocytosis:** Variation in RBC shape
- **Hypersegmented neutrophils:** Measurement of neutrophil hypersegmentation is sensitive and readily available
- **Howell-Jolly bodies:** May be present

### Laboratory Findings
- **Serum folate:** ↓ Decreased. If < 3 mcg/L or ng/mL (< 7 nmol/L), deficiency is likely. Serum folate reflects folate status unless intake has recently increased or decreased.
- **Erythrocyte (RBC) folate:** ↓ Decreased. Better reflects tissue stores if intake has changed. A level of < 140 mcg/L or ng/mL (< 305 nmol/L) indicates inadequate status.
- **Serum vitamin B12:** Measured to differentiate from vitamin B12 deficiency
- **Homocysteine:** ↑ Elevated (suggests tissue folate deficiency; also affected by vitamin B12 and vitamin B6 levels, renal insufficiency, and genetic factors)
- **Methylmalonic acid (MMA):** Normal. A normal MMA level may differentiate folate deficiency from vitamin B12 deficiency because MMA rises in B12 deficiency but not in folate deficiency.

### Diagnosis/Recognition
Diagnosis requires laboratory testing to confirm. Complete blood count and serum vitamin B12 and folate levels are done. CBC may indicate megaloblastic anemia indistinguishable from that of vitamin B12 deficiency.

### Clinical Features
- **Glossitis**
- **Diarrhea**
- **Depression**
- **Confusion**
- Anemia may develop insidiously and, because of compensatory mechanisms, be more severe than symptoms suggest
- **Folate deficiency during pregnancy:** Increases the risk of fetal neural tube defects and perhaps other brain defects
- **Note:** Neurologic lesions seen in B12 deficiency do not occur in folate deficiency

### Treatment
- **Supplemental oral folate:** 400–1000 mcg orally once a day replenishes tissues and is usually successful even if deficiency resulted from malabsorption
- **Normal requirement:** 400 mcg/day
- **Pregnant women:** Recommended dietary allowance (RDA) is 600 mcg/day. All women who are pregnant or who plan to or may become pregnant should take folic acid supplements, 400–800 mcg orally once a day.
- **Women who have had a fetus or infant with a neural tube defect:** Recommended dose is 4000 mcg/day, started 3 months before conception (if possible) and continued until 12 weeks of gestation
- **Upper limit:** 1000 mcg; higher daily doses (up to 4 mg) are recommended for women who have had a baby with a neural tube defect
- **CRITICAL CAUTION:** In patients with megaloblastic anemia, vitamin B12 deficiency must be ruled out before treating with folate. If vitamin B12 deficiency is present, folate supplementation can alleviate the anemia but does not reverse, and may even worsen, neurologic deficits.
- Folate is essentially nontoxic. Women taking both oral contraceptives and antiseizure medications may need to take folate supplements to maintain birth control effectiveness.

### Key Clues
- Macrocytosis (MCV > 100 fL) + hypersegmented neutrophils + macro-ovalocytes
- Low serum folate and low RBC folate
- Normal methylmalonic acid (distinguishes from vitamin B12 deficiency)
- Elevated homocysteine
- Glossitis, diarrhea, depression, confusion
- NO neurologic deficits (unlike B12 deficiency)
- Pregnancy-related neural tube defect risk

**Source:** Merck Manual — Folate Deficiency (Professional, Aug 2024) and Megaloblastic Macrocytic Anemias (Professional, Mar 2025)

## ANEMIA DUE TO EXCESSIVE BLEEDING (BLOOD LOSS ANEMIA)

### Identity
- **Official name:** Anemia Due to Excessive Bleeding
- **Alternative names:** Blood loss anemia; hemorrhagic anemia
- **Short definition:** Anemia resulting when loss of red blood cells exceeds production of new red blood cells
- **Main classification(s):** Normocytic (acute) or Microcytic (chronic); Blood loss; Acquired
- **Inherited or acquired:** Acquired

### Cause
Excessive bleeding is the most common cause of anemia. Causes by type:

**Rapid blood loss:**
- Injury
- Surgery
- Childbirth
- Ruptured blood vessel

**Chronic blood loss:**
- Ulcers in the stomach or small intestine
- Polyps in the large intestine
- Cancers in the large intestine
- Kidney tumors or bladder tumors (may cause blood to be lost in the urine)
- Heavy menstrual bleeding
- Nosebleeds
- Hemorrhoids

Chronic blood loss is far more common than sudden loss of blood. Small amounts of bleeding may not be noticed (occult blood loss).

### Pathophysiology
When blood is lost, the body quickly pulls water from tissues outside the bloodstream in an attempt to keep the blood vessels filled. As a result, the blood is diluted, and the hematocrit is reduced. Eventually, increased production of red blood cells by the bone marrow may correct the anemia. However, over time, bleeding reduces the amount of iron in the body, so that the bone marrow is not able to increase production of new red blood cells to replace those lost.

### RBC/CBC Characteristics
- **MCV:** Variable. In rapid blood loss, initially normocytic. In chronic blood loss, may become microcytic as iron stores are depleted.
- **Hemoglobin/Hematocrit:** Reduced (hematocrit is reduced as blood is diluted)
- **Reticulocyte count:** Initially increased as bone marrow attempts to compensate; may become inadequate in chronic blood loss as iron is depleted

### Peripheral Smear/Morphology
Not specifically described by Merck for this entity.

### Laboratory Findings
- **CBC:** Detects anemia
- **Stool and urine tests:** For blood, to identify source of bleeding
- **Iron studies:** May show iron deficiency in chronic blood loss as iron is lost with bleeding

### Diagnosis/Recognition
Doctors do blood tests to detect anemia when people describe symptoms of anemia, have noticed bleeding, or both. Stool and urine are tested for blood in an effort to identify the source of bleeding. Imaging tests or endoscopy may be needed to identify the source of bleeding.

### Clinical Features
Symptoms are similar to those of other types of anemia and vary from mild to severe, depending on how much blood is lost and how rapidly it is lost.

**Rapid blood loss:**
- Symptoms may be severe initially, especially if anemia develops rapidly
- Blood pressure falls because the amount of fluid left in the blood vessels is insufficient
- The body's oxygen supply is drastically reduced because the number of oxygen-carrying red blood cells has decreased so quickly
- Dizziness upon sitting or standing after lying down (orthostatic hypotension) is common
- Losing one-third of blood volume over several hours or less can be fatal
- Either problem may lead to a heart attack, stroke, or death

**Chronic blood loss:**
- The anemia generally causes fewer symptoms and may cause only fatigue and weakness, or no symptoms at all
- People may notice black, tarry stools if they have bleeding from the stomach or small intestine
- Bleeding from the kidneys or bladder may cause red or brown urine
- Females may notice long, heavy menstrual periods
- Some disorders that cause chronic bleeding, such as stomach ulcers, cause abdominal discomfort
- Other disorders, such as diverticulosis and intestinal cancers and polyps at an early stage, cause no symptoms

### Treatment
- **Stopping bleeding:** For large or rapid blood loss, the source of bleeding must be found and the bleeding stopped
- **Transfusion of red blood cells:** May be needed for rapid or large blood loss
- **Iron supplements:** Most people who have anemia due to bleeding need to take iron supplements, usually tablets, for several months. Because iron, which is required to produce red blood cells, is lost as a result of bleeding.
- **Intravenous iron:** Sometimes given
- With slow or small blood loss, the body may produce enough red blood cells to correct the anemia without the need for blood transfusions once the bleeding is stopped

### Key Clues
- History of bleeding (acute or chronic)
- Rapid blood loss: hypotension, orthostatic hypotension, dizziness
- Chronic blood loss: fatigue, weakness, pallor; may be occult
- Iron deficiency develops in chronic blood loss
- Low hematocrit with evidence of bleeding source
- Positive stool or urine blood tests
